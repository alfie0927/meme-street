Two minutes on a chart

A lesson on risk, reward and why nobody knows the next price
Ages14 to 18
Time45 to 50 minutes
FitsPersonal finance, economics, business
You needOne computer, a projector, internet

Students will be able to

  • read a candle chart: what one candle shows and what its colour means
  • explain buying, selling and short selling in plain words
  • explain why a pattern in past prices does not tell you the next move
  • name one way feelings change a money decision

Key words

  • Share: a small part of a company that can be bought and sold
  • Candle: one bar of a price chart: where the price opened, closed, and its high and low
  • Short: a trade that gains when the price falls
  • Return: the gain or loss as a percentage of the money you started with

The lesson

TimeWhat happens
5 min
Starter		Draw five candles on the board, each one higher than the last. Ask: “Will the sixth be up or down?” Hands up for each. Write the two counts on the board and do not say who is right.
8 min
Explain		A candle is one slice of time. Green: the price closed higher than it opened. Red: lower. The thin lines show the highest and lowest price in that slice. Buy if you think the price will rise, sell to take your gain or loss. Short if you think it will fall (in the game, pressing Sell with no shares opens a short). Everyone starts with 1,000 MB of practice money. This game charges no fee; real trading does.
10 min
Play as a class		Open memestreet.net/daily?ref=lesson on the projector and choose one student as the trader at the keyboard. Press Play: the chart appears and a new candle prints every second for two minutes. About every 20 seconds, call a vote: buy, sell, short or wait. The trader does what most hands say. When the time is up, read out the class’s return and where it ranks among everyone who played the same chart today.
12 min
Discuss		Go back to the counts on the board. In the game, did a run of rising candles always keep going? Then ask: Why did we buy when we did? Did we follow the chart, or each other? When the price fell after we bought, what did the room want to do, and why? How would a fee on every trade have changed our result? If we played the same way tomorrow, on a new chart, would we get the same result?
8 min
Write		Each student answers three questions on paper: (1) What does a red candle show? (2) How does a short trade gain? (3) “The price went up five times in a row, so it will go up again.” Explain what is wrong with this.
At homeA new chart starts every day at midnight UTC. Play it for five days and keep a decision diary: for every trade, one sentence on why. After five days, read the diary and underline every reason that was a feeling, not a fact. The reading lessons at memestreet.net/lessons cover candles, risk and short selling.

Answers to the three written questions

  • (1) In that slice of time the price closed lower than it opened.
  • (2) A short sells first, at today’s price, and buys back later. If the price has fallen, the difference is the gain. If it has risen, the difference is the loss.
  • (3) Five rises do not cause a sixth. A run can end on any candle, and the chart cannot say which. In real trading, acting on every run also costs a fee each time.

Before you teach it

  • Practice money only. MB have no cash value. Nothing can be won, lost or paid out. Every company and chart is invented, so nobody can look up the answer.
  • No accounts are needed for the class game on one screen, and no student gives a name or any other detail. On separate devices each student would need an account (age 13 or over; a name and a password, no email), because only a few visitors a day can play from one internet address and a school usually shares one. Check your school’s rules first.
  • The site has a shop that sells extra practice money and memberships. Nothing in this lesson uses it.
  • This is not investment advice. The aim is to see how hard prices are to predict, not to teach a way to beat the market.
