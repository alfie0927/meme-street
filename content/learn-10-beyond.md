# Beyond stocks {beyond}
Bonds and yields, commodities, currencies, crypto, property, and index funds against picking stocks.

## Bonds and yields {bonds} [6]
SUMMARY: What a bond is, what its yield means, and why bond prices fall when rates rise.
A **bond** is a loan you make to a government or a company. In return it pays you a fixed interest payment (the **coupon**) every year and gives you back the face value at the end (**maturity**).

### Yield
The **yield** is the return you get for buying the bond at today's price. A bond with a face value of 100 and a 5% coupon pays 5 a year. Bought at 100, it yields 5%. Bought at 90, it still pays 5 a year, plus 10 more at maturity: a higher yield.

### Why prices fall when rates rise
Say you own that 5% bond, and interest rates rise so new bonds pay 6%. Nobody will pay 100 for your 5 a year when a new bond pays 6, so the price of yours falls until its yield matches: rates up, prices down. The longer until maturity, the bigger the effect (**duration**): a 30-year bond can lose a fifth or more of its value when rates jump a couple of points; a 1-year bond barely moves.

### Risk
- **Government bonds** of strong countries: almost no risk of not being paid, so they are safe havens in panics.
- **Corporate bonds**: pay more, with credit risk. High-yield ("junk") bonds pay most and default most.
- **Inflation** is the bondholder's enemy: fixed payments buy less.

## Commodities {commodities} [6]
SUMMARY: Oil, gas, metals and crops: what drives them, and how they differ from shares.
**Commodities** are raw materials: energy (crude oil, natural gas), metals (gold, copper), and agricultural goods (wheat, corn). They have no earnings and pay no dividends; their prices come from **supply and demand**.

### What drives them
- **Crude oil**: global growth (demand), production decisions by big producers, wars and supply disruptions, stockpiles.
- **Natural gas**: weather (cold winters, hot summers), storage levels, pipelines. Very volatile, with strong seasons.
- **Copper**: industry and construction; called "Dr Copper" because it tends to rise and fall with the world economy.
- **Wheat and other crops**: harvests, weather, droughts, export bans.
- **Gold**: no industrial story to speak of. It is a store of value: it tends to rise in fear, with high inflation, when real interest rates fall, and when the dollar weakens.

### Commodities and shares
Oil companies tend to follow oil, miners follow metals, airlines suffer when fuel is dear. A commodity spike can lift one industry and hurt another on the same day.

## Currencies and forex {forex} [6]
SUMMARY: How currency pairs work, what moves them, and why forex is a leveraged game.
The **foreign exchange (forex)** market is the biggest in the world: trillions of dollars a day. Currencies trade in **pairs**: EUR/USD at 1.10 means one euro costs 1.10 dollars. Buying EUR/USD is betting the euro rises against the dollar.

### What moves currencies
- **Interest rates**: higher rates attract money, so a currency tends to rise when its central bank raises rates more than expected (the "carry").
- **Growth and trade**: strong economies and exports support a currency.
- **Risk mood**: in panics, money runs to the US dollar, the Swiss franc and the Japanese yen.
- **Inflation**: high inflation erodes a currency over time.

### Pips and leverage
Major pairs move less than 1% on most days, so forex is traded with high leverage (often 30 to 1 for retail traders in Europe, more elsewhere). A small move against a large position can wipe out an account, and most retail forex traders lose money.

### Currency risk in shares
Buying foreign shares is also a bet on the currency: a 10% gain in a foreign stock can be wiped out by a 10% fall in its currency.

## Crypto: what is different {crypto} [6]
SUMMARY: Digital assets, how they differ from shares, and the extra risks.
**Cryptocurrencies** (Bitcoin, Ether and thousands of others) are digital tokens recorded on a **blockchain**: a shared ledger kept by many computers instead of one bank.

### How they differ from shares
- **No earnings or cash flow** behind most tokens: their price is what the next buyer will pay, driven by adoption, scarcity and sentiment.
- **Trading never stops**: 24 hours a day, every day.
- **Volatility** is extreme: falls of 50% to 80% from a peak have happened several times to the biggest coins, and many small tokens go to zero.
- **Regulation** is patchy and changing.

### The extra risks
- **Custody**: lose the keys to a wallet and the coins are gone; exchanges have collapsed and taken customers' money with them.
- **Scams and rug pulls**: new tokens launched, hyped and abandoned by their creators.
- **Leverage**: crypto exchanges offer 50x or 100x, and liquidations cascade.
- **Stablecoins** are meant to hold a fixed value (one dollar) and are only as good as the reserves behind them.

### Crypto companies
Exchanges, miners and wallet makers are ordinary companies whose fortunes follow crypto prices, usually with even bigger swings.

## REITs, and index funds against stock picking {reits-index} [6]
SUMMARY: Property through the stock market, and why most investors are better off owning the whole market.
### REITs
A **real estate investment trust** owns property (offices, warehouses, flats, shopping centres, data centres) and must pay out most of its rental income as dividends. It lets you own property through a share, with daily liquidity. REITs behave partly like bonds (steady income, hurt by rising rates) and partly like the property they own: offices suffered when people worked from home, warehouses boomed with online shopping.

### Index funds against picking stocks
An **index fund** buys the whole market at very low cost. Most professional fund managers, who pick stocks for a living, fail to beat their index over ten years or more, after fees: in study after study, the large majority trail it. The reasons:
- Costs: active funds charge more and trade more.
- Competition: every price already reflects what thousands of professionals know.
- A few huge winners drive most of the market's gains, and most portfolios miss some of them.

### What that means for you
Owning the index is a strong default for long-term money. Picking stocks or trading can make sense as a skill you are building, with money you can afford to lose, if you measure honestly whether you beat simply holding the index.
