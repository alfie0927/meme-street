# Fundamentals {fundamentals}
What a company earns, owns and owes, how it is valued, dividends and dilution, earnings season, and why sectors are valued differently.

## The three financial statements {statements} [7]
SUMMARY: The income statement, the balance sheet and the cash flow statement, in plain words.
Public companies publish three statements every quarter. Together they answer: is it profitable, what does it own and owe, and is cash really coming in?

### The income statement (profit and loss)
What came in and what went out over the period, from the top line to the bottom line:
- **Revenue** (sales): everything customers paid.
- minus the cost of making what was sold: **gross profit**.
- minus running costs (staff, rent, research, marketing): **operating profit**.
- minus interest and tax: **net income**, the bottom line.

### The balance sheet
A snapshot of what the company **owns** (assets: cash, buildings, stock in the warehouse, money owed to it) and what it **owes** (liabilities: debt, bills to pay). The difference is **shareholders' equity**: what would be left for owners if everything were sold and every debt paid.

### The cash flow statement
Profit is an accounting figure; cash is what pays the bills. This statement shows cash from operations, cash spent on investment (factories, acquisitions) and cash from financing (borrowing, issuing shares, paying dividends). A company reporting profits while its operating cash flow keeps falling deserves a closer look.

## Revenue, margins, EPS, debt and cash {key-numbers} [6]
SUMMARY: The handful of numbers worth checking first, and what they tell you.
- **Revenue growth**: is the business getting bigger? Fast growth is what investors pay most for.
- **Margins**: how much of each sale is kept. **Net margin** = net income ÷ revenue. A 25% margin keeps 25p of every pound; 3% keeps 3p. Rising margins mean the business is getting more efficient or more powerful; shrinking ones mean costs or competition are biting.
- **EPS (earnings per share)** = net income ÷ shares outstanding. The profit attributable to each share, and the number earnings reports are judged on.
- **Debt**: borrowed money that must be repaid with interest whatever happens. Manageable debt helps a company grow; too much can sink it in a bad year.
- **Cash**: the cushion. Cash minus debt (**net cash**, or net debt if negative) says how much room a company has.

### Quick checks
- Debt several times revenue, with thin margins: fragile.
- Lots of cash and no debt: can survive bad years and buy rivals cheaply.
- Revenue growing but margins falling: buying growth at a cost.

## Valuation: P/E, P/S, EV/EBITDA and DCF {valuation} [8]
SUMMARY: Is a share cheap or dear? The common yardsticks, and their limits.
A great company can be a bad investment at too high a price. Valuation compares the price with what you get.

### P/E (price to earnings)
Share price ÷ EPS (or market cap ÷ net income). A P/E of 20 means you pay 20 years of today's profit. A high P/E means investors expect strong growth; a low one that they expect little, or that they are worried. It means nothing for a company that makes a loss.

### P/S (price to sales)
Market cap ÷ revenue. Useful for young companies that have sales but no profit yet.

### EV/EBITDA
**Enterprise value** = market cap + debt - cash: the price of buying the whole company including its debts. **EBITDA** is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, a rough measure of operating cash profit. EV/EBITDA lets you compare companies with very different amounts of debt.

### DCF (discounted cash flow)
The textbook way: estimate all the cash a company will make in the future and work out what it is worth today, since money later is worth less than money now. In practice the answer depends enormously on guesses about growth and the discount rate, so it is a way to think more than a precise answer.

### Compare like with like
Valuations only mean something against similar companies and against the company's own history. A software company on 35 times earnings and a steelworks on 8 can both be fairly priced.

## Dividends, buybacks and dilution {dividends-buybacks} [6]
SUMMARY: The ways companies return cash to shareholders, and the way they water shares down.
### Dividends
A share of the profit paid in cash, usually every quarter. The **dividend yield** = yearly dividends ÷ share price. A 4% yield on a 50 MB share pays 2 MB a year.

The dates that matter:
- **Declaration**: the company announces the dividend.
- **Ex-dividend date**: buy before it to get the dividend; buy on or after it and you do not. On that morning the price usually **drops by about the dividend**, because the cash is leaving the company.
- **Payment date**: the cash arrives.
Buying just before the ex-date to "collect" a dividend gains nothing: the price drop takes it back.

### Buybacks
The company buys its own shares and cancels them. Each remaining share owns a bigger slice, so EPS rises. It returns cash like a dividend, and is often more tax-efficient in real markets.

### Dilution
Issuing new shares (to raise cash, pay staff or buy another company) splits the same company into more pieces: each share's slice of the profit shrinks. A company that keeps issuing shares can grow its profit while its EPS stands still.

HERE: Dividend payers pay once a quarter, declared with the earnings report, which says whether the dividend was raised, cut or suspended. About 5 minutes later, in the regular session, the stock goes **ex-dividend**: holders receive it, shorts pay it, and the price drops by the dividend, so holding through the date has no expected gain. Companies announce **buybacks** (the share count shrinks at the next report) and **public offerings** of 1 to 4% new shares (which raise the share count, lower EPS, and do not move the price on their own).

## Earnings season: guidance, beats and misses {earnings} [7]
SUMMARY: How results are judged against forecasts, why prices react to surprises, and "buy the rumour, sell the news".
Every quarter, companies report their results. Analysts publish **forecasts** (the "consensus") beforehand, and the market judges the result against them.

### Beats and misses
- **Beat**: EPS (and revenue) above the forecast.
- **Miss**: below.
The **size of the surprise** drives the reaction, not the result itself. A record profit that misses forecasts can fall; a loss smaller than feared can rise.

### Guidance
What the company says about the next quarter or year often matters more than the quarter just gone. Strong results with weak guidance often sell off.

### Big moves on earnings day
Earnings days are often the biggest moves of a stock's quarter, and the price can gap past any stop. Options get more expensive before the report and lose that premium afterwards (the *IV crush*, in the *Derivatives* track).

### Post-earnings drift
After a big surprise, prices often keep drifting in the same direction for days as investors slowly adjust: one of the most studied patterns in finance.

### Buy the rumour, sell the news
When good news is widely expected, the price rises ahead of it, and when the news arrives there is nobody left to buy: the price falls on "good" news. The opposite happens with expected bad news.

HERE: Companies report every quarter (about every 46 real hours), only in pre-market or after-hours. The report says whether the company **beat, matched or missed**, and the price reacts to the size of the surprise: often 2.5 to 4 times an ordinary day's move. After a big surprise the price keeps drifting the same way for about three game days. ## How sectors are valued differently {sectors} [6]
SUMMARY: Why banks, chip makers, utilities and miners trade on different numbers.
Industries earn money in different ways, so the same yardstick means different things in each.
- **Technology and software**: high growth and high margins, valued on growth: high P/E and P/S are normal. Very sensitive to interest rates, because much of their value lies far in the future.
- **Banks and insurers**: valued on their book value and returns on it. They often **gain** when interest rates rise (they earn more on loans).
- **Utilities and telecoms**: steady, regulated, high dividends, low growth. Valued like bonds: they suffer when rates rise.
- **Energy and materials**: profits swing with oil, gas and metal prices. Valued on the cycle: a low P/E at the top of a cycle can be a trap.
- **Consumer staples** (food, household goods): steady demand in good times and bad. **Defensive**: they hold up in recessions.
- **Consumer discretionary** (cars, travel, luxury): **cyclical**: booms and busts with the economy.
- **Real estate (REITs)**: valued on rental income and the value of the property; sensitive to rates.
- **Crypto and young growth companies**: valued on hope and adoption; the most volatile.

### Why it matters to a trader
Industries react differently to the same news. A hotter inflation number can lift banks and sink tech on the same afternoon.

## Analysts and credit ratings {ratings} [5]
SUMMARY: Price targets, upgrades and downgrades, and what credit ratings measure.
### Analyst notes
Banks' analysts publish **ratings** (buy, hold, sell), **price targets** and earnings forecasts. Upgrades and downgrades move prices, especially from respected analysts or when they disagree with the crowd. Analysts are often late, often herd together, and rarely say "sell"; their notes are information, not instructions.

### Credit ratings
Agencies (S&P, Moody's, Fitch) grade a company's ability to repay its debts, from AAA (safest) down through AA, A, BBB (the lowest "investment grade"), then BB, B, CCC... ("junk" or "high yield") to D (in default). A downgrade raises the company's borrowing costs; a fall below BBB can force some funds to sell its bonds.

HERE: Six fictional banks rate every company on a ladder from D to AAA, based on its margin, cash, debt, volatility, beta and how safe its business is (which they can see and you have to infer). When a rating moves they publish a price-target note; about two in five give a reason ("citing leverage concerns"). Notes move the price a little. Funds, indices and commodities have no rating.
