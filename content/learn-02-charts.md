# Reading charts {charts}
Candles, timeframes, trends, support and resistance, breakouts, volume, the classic patterns and gaps: the core of technical analysis, practised on real charts.

## Candlesticks {candlesticks} [6]
SUMMARY: What a candle shows, and the shapes worth knowing.
A **candle** sums up one stretch of time (a minute, an hour, a day) in four prices:
- **Open**: the first price of the period.
- **Close**: the last.
- **High** and **low**: the extremes.

The thick **body** runs from the open to the close: green (or hollow) when the close is above the open, red when below. The thin **wicks** (or shadows) reach up to the high and down to the low.

### What the shape says
A candle is a small story of who won that period. A long green body: buyers in control from start to finish. A long upper wick: buyers pushed the price up, then sellers pushed it back down. A tiny body: neither side won.

| Candle | Shape | What it suggests |
| Doji | open and close almost equal | indecision; matters after a long move |
| Hammer | small body near the top, long lower wick, after a fall | sellers were rejected; a possible low |
| Shooting star | small body near the bottom, long upper wick, after a rise | buyers were rejected; a possible high |
| Bullish engulfing | a green body that covers the whole red body before it | buyers took over |
| Bearish engulfing | a red body that covers the whole green body before it | sellers took over |
| Marubozu | a big body with almost no wicks | one side in control throughout |

### How much to trust them
A single candle is weak evidence. It means most **at a level that matters** (a hammer on support, a shooting star at resistance) and with volume behind it. In the middle of a range, on thin volume, it means little.

## Timeframes and the price scale {timeframes} [5]
SUMMARY: Why the same stock looks different on a 1-minute and a 1-day chart, and when to use a log scale.
Every candle size tells a different story. A strong uptrend on the daily chart can contain dozens of falls on the 5-minute chart; a breakout on the 1-minute chart can be a ripple inside a range on the hourly.

### Choosing a timeframe
- Pick the **timeframe that matches how long you hold**. A trade meant to last hours is planned on hourly or 15-minute candles, not 30-second ones.
- Look at **one timeframe up** for the trend and the big levels, and **one down** to time the entry.
- Shorter timeframes have more noise relative to the move you are trying to catch, and costs matter more.

### Linear and log scale
On a **linear** scale each price step is the same height: a move from 10 to 20 looks as big as 100 to 110. On a **log** scale equal **percentage** moves are the same height: 10 to 20 (+100%) looks as big as 100 to 200. Use log for long charts and for anything that has moved a lot, or a stock's early history looks flat and its recent moves enormous.

HERE: Candle sizes run from 30 seconds to a year, in real time: a 1-hour candle is two game days. Each shows up to 2,000 candles. **Log** is a switch in the box under the chart. Remember the game's clock: a game day is 30 minutes, so the hourly chart covers weeks of game time.

TIP: Use a few candle sizes and see whether they agree. When the hourly is trending up and the 5-minute pulls back to support, the two agree on a long trade.

## Trends: higher highs and higher lows {trends} [6]
SUMMARY: How to tell an uptrend, a downtrend and a range from the swing points.
A **trend** is a series of swings in one direction. Prices never move in straight lines; they push and pull back. The pattern of those swings defines the trend:
- **Uptrend**: each swing high is higher than the last (**higher highs**) and each pullback stops higher than the last (**higher lows**).
- **Downtrend**: **lower highs** and **lower lows**.
- **Range** (sideways): highs and lows at about the same prices.

### Reading it
Mark the last few swing highs and lows. Two higher highs and two higher lows make an uptrend. The trend is intact until the structure breaks: in an uptrend, the first close below the last higher low is the first warning that it may be ending.

### Trading with it
"The trend is your friend": buying pullbacks in an uptrend puts the trend's push behind you, and the last higher low gives a natural place for a stop. Fighting a trend (shorting every high in an uptrend) means betting against the push.

### How trends end
Trends end in a few ways: a **climax** (a last fast move on huge volume, a blow-off top or panic low), a slow fade where pushes get weaker and volume shrinks, or a reversal pattern such as a double top. None is certain until the structure actually breaks.

HERE: Trends here move in **legs**: an impulse then a pullback, again and again. The last push of a trend often comes on less momentum and less volume than the ones before: the divergence that warns a trend is tiring.

## Support and resistance {support-resistance} [7]
SUMMARY: Where prices tend to turn, why, and how to judge how strong a level is.
**Support** is a price where falls have stopped before: buyers stepped in. **Resistance** is a price where rises have stopped: sellers stepped in. They are not lines in the sand but zones where orders cluster.

### Why levels work
- **Memory**: traders who bought at a level and saw it fall want to sell when they get their money back there (resistance). Those who missed a bounce want to buy the next time (support).
- **Orders**: stop-losses and limit orders collect just beyond obvious levels.
- **Round numbers**: 50, 100, 150 attract orders because people think in round numbers.
- **The previous day's high, low and close**, and long moving averages, are watched by everyone.

### How strong is a level?
- **Touches**: a level that has turned the price three times is stronger than one that turned it once.
- **Confluence**: a swing high on a round number on the 50-day average is stronger than each alone.
- **The trend**: support in an uptrend holds more often than support in a downtrend.
- **Recent tests**: a level tested again and again in a short time is being worn down, and tends to break.

### Role reversal
A broken resistance often becomes support on the way back (and broken support becomes resistance). The *retest* of a broken level is a classic entry.

### Levels fail
No level always holds. Strong levels hold a little more often than not; weak ones a little less. That is why every trade at a level has a stop just beyond it.

## Ranges and breakouts {ranges-breakouts} [6]
SUMMARY: Prices stuck between support and resistance, the squeeze before the move, and breakouts that run or fail.
A **range** is a market going sideways between support and resistance. Ranges are where the market makes up its mind; they end in a **breakout**, when the price escapes one side.

### The squeeze
Late in a range the candles often shrink and volume dries up: the market goes quiet. Volatility comes in waves, and a very quiet market tends to be followed by a lively one. Bollinger Bands narrowing to a "squeeze" is the classic picture.

### Trading the breakout
- Wait for a **close** beyond the level, not just a wick.
- Look for **volume** growing on the break.
- The stop goes back **inside the range**: if the price returns there, the breakout has failed.
- A first target is the **height of the range** added to the breakout point.

### Fake breakouts
Many breakouts fail: the price pokes out, triggers everyone's buy orders, and falls back inside. Traders call it a **fakeout** or **bull trap**. Some wait for a **retest** (the price comes back to the broken level and holds) before getting in, accepting a worse price for more confirmation.

HERE: Ranges here always end in a breakout, more often upwards in a rising market and downwards in a falling one, and the last part of a range is quieter: the squeeze before the move. A break that is false tends to come on somewhat less volume than a real one, but the two overlap, so volume is a hint, never a tell.

## Volume: confirming moves and spotting fakes {volume} [5]
SUMMARY: What volume adds to a price chart, and the patterns traders read in it.
Price tells you what happened; volume tells you how many people took part. The same price move means more when many shares changed hands.

### What traders look for
- **Rising volume in the trend's direction**: healthy. Impulses on high volume, pullbacks on low volume.
- **Breakouts on high volume**: more likely to be real.
- **New highs on falling volume**: fewer buyers are joining in; the trend may be tiring.
- **Climax volume**: an enormous spike at the end of a long move, often marking the top or bottom (a blow-off or a panic).
- **Volume drying up** in a range: the squeeze before the breakout.

### Caveats
Volume is evidence, not proof. Real breakouts sometimes come on quiet volume, and loud breakouts sometimes fail. It shifts the odds; it does not settle them.

HERE: Volume bars sit under every chart (green for a candle that closed up, red for down), and the free indicators include the 20-candle volume average drawn over them. Indices and funds have no volume of their own. Volume here is busier in trend impulses than pullbacks, bigger on breakouts and climaxes, and quieter late in a range, as in real markets.

## Reversal patterns {reversal-patterns} [7]
SUMMARY: Double tops and bottoms, head and shoulders, and wedges: shapes that often mark a trend's end.
Chart patterns are shapes the swings make, repeated often enough to have names. **Reversal patterns** appear at the end of a trend.

### Double top and double bottom
Two highs at about the same price with a dip between them. The low of the dip is the **neckline**. The pattern is complete only when the price **closes below the neckline**; until then it is just two highs. A **double bottom** is the mirror image after a fall.

### Head and shoulders
Three highs: a left shoulder, a higher head, and a right shoulder at about the height of the left. The lows between them form the neckline. A close below the neckline completes it. The **inverse head and shoulders** marks a bottom.

### Wedges
A **rising wedge**: the price makes higher highs and higher lows, but the lows rise faster than the highs, so the range narrows as it climbs. The push is weakening, and it usually breaks downwards. A **falling wedge** is the mirror image, usually breaking upwards.

### Trading a pattern
- **Entry**: a close beyond the trigger (the neckline).
- **Stop**: beyond the other side of the pattern (above the right shoulder, above the second top).
- **Target**: the **measured move**: the pattern's height added to the trigger.

### How often they work
Patterns are probabilities. Some complete and reach their target; some break out and then turn back (they are "busted"); many never break out at all. Wait for the trigger and keep the stop.

## Continuation patterns {continuation-patterns} [7]
SUMMARY: Flags, triangles, channels and the cup and handle: pauses inside a trend.
**Continuation patterns** are pauses inside a trend, after which it often resumes.

### Flags
A sharp move (the **pole**), then a small, orderly drift against it (the **flag**), giving back less than half the pole. A **bull flag** follows a rise; a **bear flag** a fall. A break of the flag's edge in the trend's direction completes it, and the classic target is the pole's height added to the breakout.

### Triangles
- **Ascending**: flat highs, rising lows. Buyers are getting more eager; it usually breaks up.
- **Descending**: flat lows, falling highs. It usually breaks down.
- **Symmetric**: falling highs and rising lows. It tends to break the way the trend into it was going.
Volume usually shrinks as the triangle narrows, then expands on the break.

### Channels
Two parallel lines containing a trend: buy near the lower line in a rising channel, sell near the upper. A close outside the channel is a warning.

### Cup and handle
A rounded bottom (the cup), a rise back to the old high, a small pullback (the handle), then a break above the rim.

### Not every pause continues
A flag or triangle that never breaks out was just a pause, and some break the wrong way. The rules are the same as for any pattern: wait for the close beyond the trigger, put the stop beyond the other side, aim for the measured move.

## Gaps and fair value gaps {gaps} [5]
SUMMARY: Why prices jump, the kinds of gap, and the gaps traders expect to be revisited.
A **gap** is a jump between one candle's close and the next one's open, with no trading in between. On daily charts they come at the open, after news or earnings released while the market was quiet.

### Kinds of gap
- **Common gap**: small, inside a range, usually filled quickly (the price comes back to the previous close).
- **Breakaway gap**: out of a range on big news and volume, the start of a new move; often not filled for a long time.
- **Runaway gap**: in the middle of a strong trend, as late buyers rush in.
- **Exhaustion gap**: a last jump at the end of a trend, soon filled as the move collapses.

"Gaps always fill" is a myth: many do, many do not, and a gap that fills may take months.

### Fair value gaps
On any timeframe, three candles where the first one's high is below the third one's low (in a fast rise) leave a **fair value gap**: a stretch the price moved through so fast that little traded there. Traders watch for the price to come back into it, where it often finds support or resistance.

HERE: The regular session opens with a jump every game day, and earnings come out in the quiet sessions, so a game-day view shows the gap between one close and the next open. The real-time charts are continuous, but fast moves leave fair value gaps on every candle size. The Daily Chart's after-game breakdown marks fair value gaps the chart tested.

## Reading several timeframes {multi-timeframe} [5]
SUMMARY: Using a higher timeframe for direction and a lower one for timing.
Most traders read at least two timeframes:
- The **higher** one (say, hourly) sets the direction and the big levels.
- The **lower** one (say, 5-minute) times the entry and places a tight, logical stop.

### An example
The hourly chart is in an uptrend, and the price has pulled back to a support level that has held twice. On the 5-minute chart, the fall into the level slows, a higher low forms, and the price breaks a small downtrend line. That is an entry in the direction of the bigger trend, at a level, with a stop just below the small higher low.

### When they disagree
If the hourly is falling and the 5-minute is rising, the 5-minute rally is a pullback inside a downtrend. Trading it long is trading against the bigger picture: smaller size, closer target, or no trade.

### Do not hunt for a timeframe that agrees
Flicking through candle sizes until one confirms what you already want to do is a trap. Pick your two timeframes before you look.
