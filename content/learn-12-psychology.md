# Psychology {psychology}
The biases that cost traders more than any bad indicator, judging decisions by process, journals and routines, and knowing when to stop.

## The biases that cost money {biases} [8]
SUMMARY: FOMO, revenge trading, loss aversion, overconfidence, sunk cost and recency bias.
Most trading mistakes are not about not knowing what to do. They are about not doing it, because a human brain is built for other things.

### FOMO (fear of missing out)
A stock is flying and everyone is talking about it, so you buy, late, at the top of a candle, with no plan. FOMO trades are bought after the move, at the worst risk-to-reward, usually just before the pullback. **Cure**: if you missed it, you missed it. There is always another setup.

### Revenge trading
After a loss, you trade again immediately, bigger, to "win it back". The decision is about your feelings, not the chart. A bad loss followed by revenge trades is how most blown-up accounts happen. **Cure**: after a big loss or a set number of losses, stop for the day.

### Loss aversion
Losses hurt about twice as much as equal gains feel good. So traders hold losers too long (hoping to avoid admitting the loss) and sell winners too early (to lock in the good feeling): the exact opposite of "cut losses, let winners run". **Cure**: stops and targets set before the trade, and left alone.

### Overconfidence
A few wins and it all seems easy, so the size goes up and the stops go out. Wins in a rising market feel like skill. **Cure**: keep the same risk per trade, and judge yourself on many trades, not a good week.

### Sunk cost
"I have already lost 30% on this, I cannot sell now." The money is gone whether you sell or not; the only question is whether you would buy it today at this price. If not, why hold it?

### Recency bias
The last few trades feel like the whole truth. Three losses in a row and the strategy "does not work"; three wins and it is "perfect". **Cure**: judge strategies over 50 to 100 trades.

### Confirmation bias
Once you own a stock, you notice the news that agrees with you and ignore the rest. **Cure**: before every trade, write down what would prove you wrong.

Q: You have lost three trades today and feel you must make it back now. What is this?
+ The urge to revenge trade: time to stop for the day
- A good time to double the size

WHY: Trading to recover a loss is driven by emotion, and usually makes it worse.
Q: Why do traders tend to sell winners early and hold losers?
- Winners are riskier
+ Loss aversion: losses hurt more than gains please, so people avoid realising them
- It is the best strategy
WHY: The pain of admitting a loss keeps losers open; the relief of a gain closes winners too soon.
Q: You are down 40% on a stock. What is the right question?
- How do I get back to break-even?
+ Would I buy it today, at this price, with what I know now?
- How long until it recovers?
WHY: What you paid is a sunk cost; only the future matters for the decision.

## Process over outcome {process} [6]
SUMMARY: Good decisions can lose and bad ones can win: judge the decision, not the result.
In a world with randomness, a good decision can have a bad outcome and a bad one a good outcome. Running a red light and arriving safely does not make it a good decision.

| | Good outcome | Bad outcome |
| Good process | deserved success | bad luck: do it again |
| Bad process | dumb luck: dangerous | deserved loss |

The dangerous corner is **bad process, good outcome**: a reckless trade that worked teaches you to do it again, until it does not.

### What a good process looks like
- A setup you have tested and understand.
- A stop where the idea is wrong, and a size from the stop.
- A target the chart supports, with reward bigger than risk.
- The plan followed, whatever happens.

### Score yourself on the process
After each trade, ask: did I follow my rules? A trade that followed the rules and hit its stop is a **good trade**. A trade that broke the rules and made money is a **bad trade**. Over hundreds of trades, good process is the only thing that adds up.

- A bad trade

- A reason to abandon the plan
WHY: Following a sound plan is the job; losses are part of any edge.

## Trading journals and routines {journal} [6]
SUMMARY: Writing down every trade, reviewing them, and the routines that keep you consistent.
### The journal
For every trade, write down:
- The **date**, the stock, long or short, the size.
- **Why**: the setup, the timeframe, what you saw.
- The **plan**: entry, stop, target, and the risk in R.
- The **result** in R, and whether you followed the plan.
- How you **felt**: bored, scared, overconfident, rushed.

### Reviewing it
Once a week, read the journal back:
- Which setups made money and which lost it?
- Which mistakes repeat? (Moving stops, entering late, trading out of boredom.)
- Did losses come from bad luck or broken rules?
Patterns jump out that you would never see trade by trade: "every trade I took in the first minute of the session lost", "my shorts are all losers".

### Routines
- **Before**: check the calendar (releases, earnings), the market's direction, your levels; decide what you will and will not trade.
- **During**: only the setups in your plan.
- **After**: journal, review, stop.

+ Repeating patterns in your mistakes and your best setups
- The future price of a stock

WHY: Reviewing many trades together shows patterns a single trade never does.

- Only the profit
+ The setup, the plan, the result in R, whether the plan was followed, and how you felt

WHY: Context and behaviour are what make a journal useful for improving.

## Knowing when to stop {stopping} [5]
SUMMARY: Daily loss limits, signs of tilt, and why not trading is a position too.
### A daily loss limit
Decide in advance: if the day's losses reach a set amount (say 3% of the account, or three losing trades), you stop until tomorrow. The limit is set when you are calm, for the moment you will not be.

### Signs you are on tilt
- Trading faster and bigger than usual.
- Taking setups that are not in your plan.
- Moving or removing stops.
- Feeling angry, desperate or invincible.
- Staring at a position, willing it to turn.
Any of these: close the screen.

### Not trading is a position
No trade means no loss. Choppy, directionless markets, the minutes around a big release, days when you are tired or upset: sitting out is a decision, and often the best one available.

### Stopping when it goes well, too
After a big winning day, the temptation is to keep going while "hot". Overconfidence after wins is as costly as revenge after losses. Some traders stop after reaching a daily target.

HERE: The market here never closes and a game day is 30 minutes, so there is always another session. That makes a personal stopping rule more important, not less.

Q: What is a daily loss limit for?
+ To stop trading before emotion turns a bad day into a disaster
- To guarantee a profit
- To meet a legal requirement
WHY: Set while calm, it protects you from decisions made on tilt.
Q: Which is a sign of being on tilt?
- Following your plan exactly
+ Trading bigger and faster after losses
- Journaling your trades
WHY: Escalating size and pace after losses is the classic tilt pattern.
