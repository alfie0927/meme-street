# Macro and the economy {macro}
Inflation, central banks and interest rates, the data that moves markets, why the surprise matters, the business cycle, risk-on and risk-off, and the yield curve.

## Inflation and central banks {inflation} [6]
SUMMARY: What inflation is, why central banks fight it, and how they do it.
**Inflation** is the rate at which prices in general rise. It is measured by the **consumer price index (CPI)**: the price of a fixed basket of everyday goods and services, compared with a year before. "CPI 3.4%" means that basket costs 3.4% more than a year ago.

### Why it matters
- A little inflation (around 2% a year) is normal and is what most central banks aim for.
- High inflation eats savings and wages, and makes planning hard.
- Falling prices (deflation) can be worse: people delay spending, and debts get heavier.

### Central banks
A **central bank** (the Federal Reserve in the US, the Bank of England, the European Central Bank) sets the short-term **interest rate**, the price of borrowing money for the whole economy.
- Inflation too high: **raise rates**. Borrowing gets dearer, spending and investment slow, prices cool.
- Economy too weak: **cut rates**. Borrowing gets cheaper, spending picks up.
Its decisions are announced on scheduled dates, and markets spend weeks guessing them.

### "Hawkish" and "dovish"
A **hawkish** central bank worries most about inflation and leans towards higher rates; a **dovish** one worries most about growth and jobs and leans towards lower rates. Markets read every word of their statements for hints.

HERE: The economy here has growth, unemployment, inflation and a **policy rate** set by *the Reserve Board*. The board follows a rule: it raises rates when inflation runs above target or the economy runs hot, and cuts when they cool. Rate decisions come eight times a game year (about every 23 real hours), priced in advance as a probability ("a 0.25-point rise expected (72%)"). Consumer prices (CPI) are published about every 15 real hours. All of it is on the **Calendar**.

## How interest rates affect stocks {rates} [6]
SUMMARY: The channels from the policy rate to share prices, and which companies feel it most.
Higher interest rates pull share prices down through several channels:
- **Discounting**: a share is worth its future profits in today's money. Higher rates make future money worth less today, so companies whose profits lie far in the future (young growth companies) lose the most value.
- **Competition**: when savings accounts and bonds pay 5%, shares have to offer more to attract money.
- **Costs**: companies with debt pay more interest; consumers with mortgages and loans spend less.
- **Growth**: higher rates slow the economy, and profits with it.

Lower rates work the other way, which is why markets often rally when a central bank turns dovish.

### Who wins and who loses
- Hurt most: long-duration growth (technology, young companies), property, utilities (valued like bonds), heavily indebted companies.
- Can benefit: **banks** (they earn more on loans) and companies sitting on cash.

### It is the change in expectations that matters
If a rate rise was fully expected, it is already in prices. Markets move when the decision, or the guidance about future decisions, differs from what was priced.

HERE: Each industry here has its own sensitivity to rates: banks gain when rates surprise upwards; property, technology, crypto and utilities lose. The whole market tends to fall on a rates surprise upwards and rise on one downwards, through each company's beta.

Q: Rates rise more than expected. Which company is likely hurt most?
+ A young software company with profits expected years from now
- A bank
- A company with lots of cash and no debt
WHY: Higher rates shrink the present value of far-off profits the most.
Q: A rate rise was 95% expected and happens. What usually happens to the market?
- A big fall
+ Little reaction: it was already priced in
- A big rise, always
WHY: Prices move on surprises; an expected decision is no surprise.

## Jobs, GDP, PMI and retail sales {data-releases} [6]
SUMMARY: The big economic releases, what each measures, and how often they come.
| Release | What it measures | Strong reading means |
| Jobs report (payrolls, unemployment) | how many jobs were created; the unemployment rate | a hot economy; possibly higher rates |
| Weekly jobless claims | new claims for unemployment benefit | fewer claims is strong |
| GDP | the total output of the economy; growth per quarter | expansion |
| PMI (purchasing managers' index) | a survey of businesses; above 50 is expanding, below 50 shrinking | business is growing |
| Retail sales | what consumers spent in shops and online | consumers are confident |
| CPI | consumer price inflation | inflation pressure; higher rates |

### Leading and lagging
Some indicators look ahead (**PMI** surveys, jobless claims), others look back (**GDP** describes a quarter already over). Markets care most about the ones that change their view of the future.

### Good news can be bad news
A very strong jobs report is good for the economy, but it may push the central bank to raise rates, which is bad for shares. On some days the market cheers strong data; on others it fears what strong data means for rates. Which reaction wins depends on what the market is worried about at the time.

HERE: Releases come on a fixed calendar: **weekly jobless claims** every 7 game days; the **PMI, jobs report, CPI and retail sales** every 30.4 game days (about 15 real hours); **GDP** every quarter; the **rate decision** eight times a game year. Each comes 150 seconds into its game day (the rate decision later), with a forecast published days before. A strong figure is read both as good for growth and as pressure for higher rates.

Q: The PMI reads 46. What does it suggest?
- Businesses are expanding fast
+ Business activity is shrinking
- Inflation is 46%
WHY: PMI above 50 means expansion, below 50 contraction.
Q: Which of these looks back rather than ahead?
+ GDP
- The PMI survey
- Weekly jobless claims
WHY: GDP reports a quarter that is already over; surveys and claims are early signals.
Q: Why can a very strong jobs report make shares fall?
- Jobs are bad for companies
+ It raises the chance of higher interest rates
- Reports are always followed by falls
WHY: Strong data can mean the central bank tightens, which weighs on share prices.

## Why the surprise moves prices {surprise} [5]
SUMMARY: Forecasts, consensus, and why markets move on the difference.
Before every release, economists publish a **forecast** (the consensus). By the time the number comes out, the market has already priced in that forecast. What moves prices is the **surprise**: the actual figure minus the forecast.
- CPI forecast 3.2%, actual 3.4%: a hot surprise. Rates may rise sooner; shares tend to fall.
- CPI forecast 3.2%, actual 3.2%: no surprise, little reaction, even though 3.2% is high.

### The size of the surprise
A surprise is judged against how wrong forecasts usually are. A 0.1-point miss on a number that is usually forecast to within 0.1 is ordinary; a 0.5-point miss is a shock.

### The first move is not always the real one
Prices often jerk one way in the first seconds after a release and then reverse, as the details are read (the headline beat, but last month was revised down). Traders call it a whipsaw. Many wait a minute before trading a release.

### Trading around a release
- Know when it is (the Calendar).
- Volatility rises sharply in the minute after: stops get hit more, and can be filled at a worse price.
- Either be out, be small, or have a stop you can live with being hit.

HERE: Each release here has a forecast that never changes once published; the figure is the economy plus noise, and the market reacts to the surprise in standard deviations, mostly within 45 seconds. About a quarter of releases **whipsaw**: the first few seconds go the wrong way. The market is a little quieter in the minutes before a release and up to nearly twice as jumpy just after.

Q: CPI comes in at 4.0%, exactly as forecast. Inflation is high. What is the likely reaction?
+ Small: there is no surprise
- A big fall, because 4% is high
- A big rise
WHY: The forecast was already in prices; only surprises move them.
Q: The first move after a release reverses within a minute. What is that called?
- A gap
+ A whipsaw
- A squeeze
WHY: A sharp move one way, then the other.
Q: You hold a position with a tight stop as a big release comes out. What is the risk?
- None
+ The jump in volatility can hit the stop, at a worse price than planned
- The release will be delayed
WHY: Releases bring sudden, large moves; tight stops are vulnerable.

## The business cycle and sector rotation {cycle} [6]
SUMMARY: Expansion, peak, contraction and recovery; bull and bear markets; which industries lead when.
Economies move in a **cycle**:
1. **Recovery**: growth picks up from a low, rates are low, profits rebound.
2. **Expansion**: strong growth, rising employment, inflation starts to build.
3. **Peak**: growth slows, inflation is high, central banks tighten.
4. **Contraction (recession)**: output falls, unemployment rises, rates are cut.

### Market phases
Stock markets move in their own phases, usually **ahead** of the economy (markets look forward):
- **Bull market**: prices rising for a long time, setbacks bought.
- **Correction**: a fast fall of 10% or so from a high, often in weeks.
- **Bear market**: a fall of 20% or more, lasting months, with sharp rallies that fail.
- **Sideways**: a long range, going nowhere.
- **Recovery**: the rebound from a bear market low, often when the news is still bad.

### Sector rotation
Different industries lead at different stages:
- Early recovery: banks, consumer discretionary, industrials.
- Expansion: technology, materials, energy.
- Late cycle and recession: consumer staples, utilities, healthcare (the **defensives**), and safe havens.
Rotation is a tendency, not a timetable, and the cycle is only obvious in hindsight.

HERE: The market here moves through hidden phases (bull, sideways, correction, bear and recovery) with realistic lengths: bull markets last longest, corrections are short and sharp, bear markets are deep. The economy shifts the odds: hot inflation with tight money makes corrections and bear markets likelier, shrinking growth a bear market, strong growth a bull market. Nothing on the site tells you the phase; you read it from the charts and the data, as in real life. Deep falls can end with a **panic low**: a sudden plunge on huge volume, a rebound, and a recovery.

Q: Which phase is a fall of 20% or more lasting months?
- A correction
+ A bear market
- A recovery
WHY: Corrections are around 10% and short; bear markets are deeper and longer.
Q: Which industries tend to hold up best in a recession?
+ Defensives: consumer staples, utilities, healthcare
- Luxury goods and travel
- Small, indebted growth companies
WHY: People keep buying food, power and medicine in bad times.
Q: Why do markets often rise while the economic news is still bad?
- They are irrational
+ They look ahead, and price the recovery before it shows in the data
- Data is always wrong
WHY: Prices reflect expectations about the future, not today's news.

## Risk-on, risk-off and safe havens {risk-on-off} [5]
SUMMARY: The market's mood swings, and where money hides when it is scared.
**Risk-on**: investors are confident and buy risky assets: growth stocks, small companies, crypto, commodities. **Risk-off**: they are scared and sell those, buying **safe havens** instead:
- **Government bonds** (of strong governments).
- **Gold**.
- Some currencies (the US dollar, the Swiss franc, the yen).
- Defensive companies.

### Reading the mood
- Small caps and high-beta stocks leading: risk-on.
- Gold and bonds rising while stocks fall: risk-off.
- Everything correlated and falling together: fear.

### Why it matters
In risk-off moves, the ordinary links between stocks break down: good companies fall with bad ones, and diversification across stocks helps less. A portfolio with some safe-haven exposure behaves better in those weeks.

HERE: **BOND** (Steady Bonds, a short-dated government bond fund) and **GLDN** (Golden Reserve, a gold trust) are the safe havens, alongside **GOLD** itself. In corrections and bear markets, stocks move together more and the high-beta companies fall hardest.

Q: Stocks fall, gold rises, government bonds rise. What is the mood?
+ Risk-off
- Risk-on
- Neutral
WHY: Money moving from risky assets into safe havens is risk-off.
Q: Which tends to fall most in a risk-off move?
- Gold
+ High-beta growth stocks
- Government bonds
WHY: The riskiest assets are sold first when fear takes over.

## The yield curve, explained simply {yield-curve} [5]
SUMMARY: Short and long interest rates, what an inverted curve means, and why people watch it.
Governments borrow for different lengths of time: 3 months, 2 years, 10 years, 30 years. The interest rate (the **yield**) on each is different. Plot them from shortest to longest and you get the **yield curve**.

### Normal: upward sloping
Usually, lending for longer pays more: a 10-year loan yields more than a 2-year one. Lenders want extra for tying their money up and for the risk of inflation over a long time.

### Inverted: downward sloping
Sometimes short rates are **above** long rates. This happens when the central bank has pushed short rates up to fight inflation, while investors expect it will have to cut them later because the economy will weaken. An inverted curve has come before most US recessions of the last 50 years, usually by a year or more, which is why it is watched so closely.

### Steepening and flattening
- **Steepening** (long rates rising faster than short): often expectations of growth and inflation ahead.
- **Flattening**: growth worries, or a central bank tightening.

### For a stock trader
The curve tells you what the bond market expects of growth and rates: an inverted curve is a reason for caution about the economy, not a timing signal for shares, which have often kept rising for months after an inversion.

HERE: The game's economy has one policy rate and no bond market with a curve, so this is background for real markets. The rate decisions, the inflation and growth data, and the safe-haven bond fund (BOND) are where rates show up here.

Q: Two-year bonds yield 5% and ten-year bonds 4%. What shape is the curve?
- Normal
+ Inverted
- Flat
WHY: Short rates above long rates: inverted.
Q: What has an inverted yield curve often come before?
+ A recession, usually a year or more later
- An immediate stock market crash
- Higher growth
WHY: It reflects expectations that rates must fall because the economy will weaken.
