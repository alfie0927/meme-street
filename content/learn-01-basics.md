# Market basics {basics}
What a share is, how exchanges, indices and funds work, what market cap, volume and the spread mean, and why prices move.

## Stocks, shares and why companies list {stocks-shares} [5]
SUMMARY: What you own when you own a share, and why a company sells them.
A **stock** is ownership of a company, divided into **shares**. If a company has 100 million shares and you own one, you own one hundred-millionth of it: of its factories, its cash, its debts and its future profits.

### Why a company sells shares
Growing costs money. A company can borrow it (and pay interest whatever happens) or sell part of itself to investors (who share in the profits and the risks). Selling shares to the public for the first time is an **initial public offering (IPO)**. After that, the shares trade between investors on a stock exchange, and the company is **listed** or **public**.

### Why shares are worth anything
A share is a claim on the company's future: the profits it will make, paid out as dividends or reinvested to grow. Its price is what buyers and sellers agree that claim is worth today. That agreement changes constantly as news arrives and as moods change, which is why prices move even when nothing about the business has.

### What owning a share gives you
- A share of the profits, if the company pays a **dividend**.
- A gain or a loss when you sell, if the price has moved.
- In real markets, a vote at the company's meetings.
- **Limited liability**: the most you can lose is what you paid. You never owe the company's debts.

Each is announced on the news wire half an hour before it trades, and lists at its announced price: there is no first-day pop to chase. Companies also leave: when the market is full, the smallest company outside the MSI 50 is bought by the biggest in its industry, for cash at the market price.

## Exchanges, indices and ETFs {exchanges-indices} [6]
SUMMARY: Where shares trade, how an index sums up a market, and how funds let you buy a whole basket at once.
An **exchange** (the New York Stock Exchange, the London Stock Exchange, Nasdaq) is the marketplace where buyers and sellers meet, with rules about how trades happen and what companies must publish.

### Indices
An **index** is one number that sums up many stocks. The S&P 500 follows 500 large US companies; the FTSE 100 the 100 largest on the London exchange. Most big indices are **weighted by market value**: a company worth ten times another moves the index ten times as much. Others are **equal-weighted** (every member counts the same) or, like the Dow Jones, weighted by price.

### Funds and ETFs
An **exchange-traded fund (ETF)** is a fund that holds a basket of assets and trades like a single share. One trade buys you the whole index, a whole industry or a theme. That is **diversification** in one click: one company's disaster is a small part of the fund.

### Leveraged and inverse funds
Some funds aim to move two or three times the index each day, or the opposite way. They are tools for short-term bets, not for holding: the *Derivatives* track explains why they lose value over time in a choppy market.

HERE: The **MSI 50** is the market's index: the 50 largest companies, weighted by market value and re-ranked every game day. There are **21 industry indices** (XTECH, XFINA, XENER...), six **themed funds** that weight their members equally (AIFX for AI and cloud, GRNX clean energy, DIVX dividend payers, FINX fintech and crypto, SPCX space, FRNT the imaginary industries), and four leveraged funds on the MSI 50: **2LMSI** and **3LMSI** (two and three times), **2SMSI** and **3SMSI** (two and three times the opposite). ## Market cap, volume and liquidity {cap-volume} [5]
SUMMARY: How big a company is, how much of it trades, and why that matters to a trader.
### Market capitalisation
**Market cap** = share price × number of shares. It is what the market says the whole company is worth. A 10 MB stock with 5 billion shares (50 billion) is far bigger than a 200 MB stock with 10 million shares (2 billion): the share price alone tells you nothing about size.

| Size | Market cap | Typical behaviour |
| Large cap | over 10 billion | steadier, widely followed, in the big indices |
| Mid cap | 2 to 10 billion | in between |
| Small cap | under 2 billion | moves more, less followed, more surprises |

### Volume
**Volume** is how many shares traded in a period: the bars under the chart. High volume means many participants agreed on the price; a move on high volume carries more conviction than one on thin volume.

### Liquidity
A **liquid** market is one where you can buy or sell a lot quickly without moving the price: lots of volume, tight spreads, many buyers and sellers. Big companies are liquid. Small ones can be **illiquid**: in a real market, a big sell order can push the price down as it fills, and the gap between the best bid and the best offer can be wide.

HERE: Market cap and the size band (small, mid or large) are on every company's page, and the market table can be sorted by market cap. Volume follows the number of shares, so the big companies trade the most. Liquidity is never a problem here: every trade fills at the price on the screen, whatever its size. In real markets it is one of the main costs of trading small companies.

## The bid, the ask and the spread {bid-ask} [5]
SUMMARY: Why there are two prices, what the spread costs you, and how Meme Street differs.
On a real exchange every stock has two prices at any moment:
- The **bid**: the highest price someone is willing to pay. You sell at the bid.
- The **ask** (or offer): the lowest price someone is willing to sell at. You buy at the ask.
- The **spread** is the gap between them.

If a stock is bid 99.98 and offered at 100.02, buying and selling straight back loses you 0.04: the spread, paid to whoever was on the other side (often a market maker, who earns a living from it). For a big, busy stock the spread is a cent or two; for a small, quiet one it can be 1% or more.

### Why it matters
The spread is a cost on every round trip, on top of commission. A strategy that trades often and aims for small gains can be destroyed by spreads alone. Traders on small stocks use **limit orders** (an order to buy at the bid or below, rather than at the ask) to avoid paying it.

HERE: Shares here have **no spread**: you buy and sell at the same displayed price, and the only cost is the 0.1% fee each way. **Options do have a bid and an ask**, as on a real exchange, about 6% of the option's value apart (wider, as a share, for cheap far-out contracts): you buy at the ask and sell at the bid. Open a stock's **Options** tab to see them.

## How prices move {price-moves} [6]
SUMMARY: Buyers and sellers, news, surprises, and why a price can fall on good news.
A price moves when buyers are more eager than sellers, or the other way round. If more people want to buy at today's price than sell, buyers must offer more, and the price rises until enough sellers appear. That is all a price chart is: the record of that tug of war.

### What changes their minds
- **News about the company**: a contract won, a product recalled, a new chief executive, an analyst's note.
- **Earnings**: the quarterly report, judged against what was expected.
- **The economy**: inflation, jobs, growth and interest rates move the whole market.
- **The crowd**: trends attract buyers, levels where the price turned before attract orders, and fear and greed swing prices further than the news alone would.

### Expectations are already in the price
Markets look ahead. If everyone expects a great quarter, the price already reflects it before the report. A great quarter that was **less great than expected** can send the price down. What moves prices is the **surprise**: the difference between what happened and what was expected.

### Random noise
Much of any minute's or hour's movement is noise: orders arriving at random. Reading a chart is about separating the moves that mean something from those that do not, and accepting that you will often be wrong about which is which.

HERE: Every headline is on the news wire as it breaks, and a company's page keeps its news log. Company stories move the price when they break, and the next earnings report then confirms them in the numbers. Economic releases come with a published forecast, and the market reacts to the surprise against it.

## Sessions: pre-market, regular and after-hours {sessions} [5]
SUMMARY: When markets trade, why the open and the close are busy, and Meme Street's 30-minute day.
Real exchanges have a **regular session** (New York: 9:30 to 16:00) and quieter **extended hours** before it (pre-market) and after it (after-hours). Fewer people trade in extended hours, so prices can jump on small orders and spreads are wider.

### The shape of a day
- **The open** is the busiest moment: orders that piled up overnight arrive at once, and the price often **gaps** away from the last close.
- **The middle** of the day is calmer.
- **The close** picks up again, as funds trade to match the closing price.

Companies usually report earnings **outside** the regular session, so the reaction shows up as a gap at the next open.

HERE: A game day is 30 real minutes, on the real clock, the same for everyone: **pre-market** for 5 minutes (:00 to :05 and :30 to :35), the **regular session** for 20 (:05 to :25 and :35 to :55) and **after-hours** for 5. Trading never stops: pre-market and after-hours are just thin, with moves about a third of their usual size. The regular session opens with a burst and an **opening jump**, calms down, and picks up again into the close. Earnings come out only in pre-market or after-hours. The header shows the session and a countdown, and a coloured banner marks the quiet sessions.

TIP: The sessions change **how much** prices move, never which way: the open is no more likely to go up than down.

## Trading halts and circuit breakers {halts} [4]
SUMMARY: Why real exchanges pause trading after extreme moves, and why Meme Street does not.
When prices move too far, too fast, real exchanges hit the brakes.

### A halt in one stock
A stock can be **halted** when news is pending (the company asks for time to publish something important) or when the price moves beyond set limits within minutes (in the US, the *limit up, limit down* bands). Trading stops for a few minutes, then reopens, often at a very different price.

### Market-wide circuit breakers
If the whole market falls far enough in a day, everything stops. In the US the levels are falls of 7% and 13% in the S&P 500 (a 15-minute pause each) and 20% (closed for the day). They are meant to give people time to think instead of panicking.

### What a halt means for you
- You cannot get out while a stock is halted.
- Your stop-loss cannot fill during the halt; when trading resumes it fills at whatever price the stock opens at, which may be far past your stop.
- The reopening is often a gap.

HERE: **There are no halts.** The game used to pause a stock after an extreme move and the whole market after a big fall in the index, but that only ever stopped players, never prices, so it was removed: you can trade straight through any crash. Gaps still happen (at the open, on earnings, on big news), and a stop-loss fills at the first price past it.
