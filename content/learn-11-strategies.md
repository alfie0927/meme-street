# Strategies and trading styles {strategies}
Trend following, mean reversion, breakouts and momentum; scalping to investing; value, growth and dividends; pairs and hedges; and why holding the market is so hard to beat.

## Trend following and momentum {trend-following} [6]
SUMMARY: Riding moves that are already under way, and why it feels bad most of the time.
**Trend following** buys what is rising and sells (or shorts) what is falling, and stays in until the trend ends. It does not try to predict; it reacts.

### How it is done
- Define the trend: price above a rising long average, or higher highs and higher lows.
- Enter on a breakout to a new high, or on a pullback to support inside the trend.
- Exit with a trailing stop (below the last higher low, a few ATR below the high, under a moving average).

### What it feels like
Trend followers usually lose on most of their trades (often 60% or more): many small losses as trends fail to start, paid for by a few big winners that run for a long time. The hard part is not the rules; it is taking ten small losses in a row and still taking the eleventh signal, which might be the big one.

### Momentum
A close cousin: buy the stocks that have risen most over the past months, sell those that have fallen most. Momentum has been one of the most persistent effects found in real markets, over long periods, with occasional brutal crashes when the market turns.

HERE: Trends here come in spells, in legs of impulses and pullbacks, and end in climaxes, slow fades or patterns. Simple trend rules (buy every 20-candle high, every moving-average cross) do not beat holding the MSI 50 here once costs are counted: what helps is reading which trends are healthy (volume, structure) and managing the exits.

## Mean reversion {mean-reversion} [6]
SUMMARY: Betting that stretched prices snap back, and when that goes badly wrong.
**Mean reversion** bets that prices which have moved too far, too fast will come back towards their average.
- Buy when the price is far below its average (oversold RSI, the lower Bollinger Band, several days of falls).
- Sell or short when it is far above.
- Exit when it gets back to the middle.

### Where it works
In **ranges**: prices bouncing between support and resistance. Mean reversion wins often (a high win rate) with small gains.

### Where it fails
In **trends**: "oversold" can get much more oversold. Buying every dip in a stock that is collapsing is how mean-reversion traders blow up: a long string of small wins, then one enormous loss. A firm stop (and staying out when the market is trending) is essential.

### Trend or range?
Mean reversion and trend following are opposites: each works where the other fails. Telling which kind of market you are in (ADX, the structure of highs and lows, the slope of the long average) matters more than the entry signal.

HERE: Ranges here pull the price back towards their middle and end in a breakout; trends push on. Buying every dip of a fixed size does not beat holding the market here, as in real markets: the edge, if any, is in recognising ranges and leaving trends alone.

## Breakout trading {breakout-trading} [5]
SUMMARY: Buying the escape from a range, and handling the many that fail.
**Breakout trading** buys when the price escapes resistance (or shorts when it breaks support), betting the break starts a new move.

### A breakout plan
- **The setup**: a clear range or pattern, ideally with a quiet squeeze at the end.
- **The trigger**: a close beyond the level, ideally on rising volume.
- **The stop**: back inside the range.
- **The target**: the range's height, a measured move, or a trailing stop.
- **The alternative**: wait for the retest of the broken level and enter there, with a tighter stop and more confirmation.

### Living with failures
Many breakouts fail. Like trend following, breakout trading wins less than half the time and relies on the ones that run. Small, quick losses on the failures are the price of being there for the big ones.

HERE: Ranges here always end in a breakout, about two thirds of which start a trend that way. Breakouts that fail tend to come on less volume, but the difference is a hint, not a rule.

## Scalping, day trading, swing trading and investing {styles} [6]
SUMMARY: The four time horizons, what each demands, and how costs change with them.
| Style | Holding time | Timeframe | Trades | Needs |
| Scalping | seconds to minutes | 30s, 1m | dozens a day | very low costs, fast reactions |
| Day trading | minutes to hours, flat by the close | 1m to 15m | a few a day | discipline, time at the screen |
| Swing trading | days to weeks | 1h to daily | a few a week | patience, wider stops |
| Investing | months to years | weekly, monthly | a few a year | fundamentals, a strong stomach |

### Costs and noise
The shorter the timeframe, the more of each move is noise and the bigger the costs relative to the move you are trying to catch. A scalper needs an edge large enough to beat fees and spreads on every trade; most do not have one. Longer styles give an edge time to show.

### Choosing a style
Choose one that fits your time, your temperament and your costs, not the one that looks most exciting. Mixing styles in one trade ("a day trade that became an investment because it went down") is a classic mistake.

HERE: Time runs fast: a game day is 30 real minutes and a game year about 7.6 real days. A "swing trade" of a few game days lasts a few real hours, and an "investment" of a game year about a week. Every trade costs 0.1% each way, so the more often you trade, the more of your result goes in fees.

## Value, growth, dividends and dollar-cost averaging {investing-styles} [6]
SUMMARY: The main ways long-term investors choose shares, and a simple way to invest regularly.
### Value investing
Buy companies that look cheap against their earnings, assets or cash flow (a low P/E, a low price to book) on the idea that the market has been too pessimistic. The risk: some are cheap for good reasons (a value trap).

### Growth investing
Buy companies growing revenue and profit fast, even at high valuations, on the idea that growth will justify the price. The risk: when growth slows or rates rise, high valuations fall hard.

### Dividend investing
Buy companies that pay steady, growing dividends, for income. The risk: a very high yield can mean the market expects a cut.

### Dollar-cost averaging
Invest a fixed amount at regular intervals (every month), whatever the price. You buy more shares when prices are low and fewer when high, never have to time the market, and avoid putting everything in at a peak. It is the simplest discipline for long-term saving.

### Quality
Many investors look for **quality**: high margins, low debt, steady growth. Quality companies rarely look cheap, and often deserve not to.

## Pairs trading and hedging {pairs-hedging} [6]
SUMMARY: Betting on one stock against another, and protecting a position you want to keep.
### Pairs trading
Find two stocks that usually move together (two banks, two oil companies). When the gap between them stretches unusually wide, buy the laggard and short the leader, betting the gap closes. The market's overall move cancels out: you are betting only on the **relative** performance.

The risk: sometimes the gap widens for a reason (one company really has changed), and the pair never comes back together. Stops apply to pairs too.

### Hedging
A **hedge** reduces a risk you do not want while keeping one you do.
- Own a company you like but fear the market will fall: short the index (or buy an index put) against it. If the market falls, the hedge offsets part of the loss; what remains is your bet on the company beating the market.
- Own shares through earnings: buy a put as insurance.
- Hold many tech stocks: short the technology index.

### The cost of hedging
Hedges cost money (borrow fees, option premiums) and cap gains. A perfectly hedged position makes nothing. Hedging is about choosing which risks to keep.

HERE: You cannot be long and short the same stock, but you can be long a company and short its industry index (XTECH, XFINA...), short the MSI 50, or buy puts. Shorts pay a borrow fee and puts cost their premium: the price of the protection.

## Why holding the market is hard to beat {hold-market} [5]
SUMMARY: Costs, competition and compounding: the honest benchmark for any strategy.
Every strategy should be compared with the simplest alternative: **buy the index and hold it**. It costs almost nothing, needs no skill, and over long periods has beaten most professionals.

### Why it is hard to beat
- **Costs**: every trade pays fees (and spreads, in real markets). Holding pays them once.
- **Missed days**: a large share of the market's long-run gain comes on a small number of big up days, often right after big down days. Traders who jump out in fear tend to miss them.
- **Competition**: prices already reflect what most people know; beating them consistently requires knowing or doing something they do not.
- **Taxes**: in real life, frequent trading creates taxable gains every year.

### What a trader is really trying to do
Beat holding the market **after costs, with less or equal risk**. Making 20% in a year when the market made 30% is underperforming; making 10% when it fell 20% is outstanding. Always compare.

### Then why trade?
To build a skill, to manage risk better than a buy-and-hold investor in a crash, or because it interests you. All valid, with money you can afford to lose, as long as you measure the result honestly against the benchmark.

HERE: The game is built so that no simple rule (buy everything, buy every dip, always short, double down, trade every level) beats holding the MSI 50 by a wide margin, costs included: the same honest test real markets set. The market drifts upwards slowly over the long run. Compare your return on the leaderboard with the MSI 50's over the same time.
