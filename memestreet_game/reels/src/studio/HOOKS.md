# Hooks

A hook is the first three seconds of a short video: what is said, what is shown and what is written, taken together.
This skill does three jobs with it: WRITE hooks for a video, DIAGNOSE why a hook failed, and REWRITE a failed hook
three different ways.

It was built from 20 of the most-watched YouTube lessons on hooks, Kallaway's Triple Hook, a study of 4,500 videos in
the finance niche and the Meme Street account's own Instagram numbers. It keeps the reasons hooks work and drops the
fill-in templates: a template copies the words of someone else's winner, a reason lets you build a hook from the facts
of the video in front of you.

## What a hook is for

A viewer in a feed is deciding, not watching. The thumb is already moving, and the video gets about as long as it
takes to recognise what is on screen. In that time the viewer asks two silent questions:

1. **What is this about?** Until that is answered they cannot choose to stay, and people leave what they cannot place.
2. **What do I get if I stay?** A reason to want the next line: a question they want answered, a stake, a result.

A hook that answers only the first is a label ("Daily Chart #6 recap"). A hook that answers only the second is a
teaser ("you won't believe this"). Both lose. Three facts shape everything else in this file:

- **Three channels.** A hook is SAID (the first words), SHOWN (frame 0 and what moves in it) and WRITTEN (the words on
  screen). By 3 seconds a voice has said about seven words, while the picture and the text could be read from the
  first frame. So the shown and written channels carry the first second, and the voice confirms.
- **The 3-second count.** Instagram reports the share who swiped away inside 3 seconds (skip rate; hold is 100 minus
  it). Only frame 0, the on-screen words and the first sentence can move that number.
- **Strangers.** A platform shows a new video to a small sample picked by topic, mostly non-followers, and extends it
  only while they stay. On the Meme Street account nearly every view is a stranger's first look.

## The principles

Seventeen ideas in five families that follow the viewer: can I place it (A), do I want the next line (B), is it mine
(C), did my eye stop (D), was I dealt with fairly (E). Each gives the idea, why it works, how it shows in the three
channels and the failure it prevents.

### A. What is this? (clarity)
**1. Topic first.** The first words, frame 0 and the first text all say what the video is about.
- Why: nobody can choose to stay with something they cannot place. Even half a sentence of delay costs viewers.
- SAID: the subject is among the first few words. SHOWN: the subject itself, recognisable at a glance. WRITTEN: the
  topic in three to six words, on screen from frame 0.
- Prevents: delay. A greeting, a warm-up, a mood shot, "this is the craziest thing I've ever seen".

**2. Plain words, one reading.** A ten-year-old gets the first sentence in one pass, in words the viewer would use.
- Why: a half-understood line gives nothing to judge by, and having to work at it feels like being made to look stupid.
- SAID: short active sentences; explain a term instead of naming it; wrap the unknown thing in a known one. SHOWN: the
  known object, not the unknown one. WRITTEN: the plain category, not the insider name.
- Prevents: confusion. Jargon, a sentence with two readings, polite words nobody says ("develop better habits").

**3. Three channels, one meaning.** What is said, shown and written mean the same thing at the same moment.
- Why: three related but different messages make the viewer stop to work out how they fit, and they miss the next line.
- SAID / SHOWN / WRITTEN: one claim, three times. The picture proves the text, and each channel works alone (muted).
- Prevents: misalignment. The voice on one topic, the footage on a second, the title on a third.

### B. Why stay? (curiosity)
**4. One open question they can guess at.** After the hook every viewer is asking the same question, has enough to
start guessing, and nothing on screen has answered it.
- Why: predicting what comes next takes the place of the urge to swipe. Answer it and the anticipation is gone; give
  too little and there is nothing to predict.
- SAID: name the topic, hold back the how or the result. SHOWN: the thing in question with its answer hidden (covered,
  blurred, not yet arrived). WRITTEN: the words that open the gap, never the answer.
- Prevents: the label (the text answers itself) and the empty teaser ("something crazy happened").

**5. Contrast with what they believe.** Interest is the distance between what the viewer already believes and what
you show them, so lead with the biggest true surprise the video holds.
- Why: a statement they already agree with needs no watching, and a broken expectation wants an explanation. It has to
  make sense at once; a surprise that only confuses is noise.
- SAID: the belief, a turn ("but", "still"), the fact. SHOWN: the picture contradicts the expectation. WRITTEN: both
  sides of the contradiction ("LANDED UPS / LOST 78%").
- Prevents: disinterest. Clear, relevant and flat; or the best fact buried at second 20.

**6. Specific and believable.** A number, a name, a date or a time limit, in a claim small enough to believe.
- Why: a specific can be pictured and checked; a promise that is too good trips the "it can't be that easy" alarm.
- SAID: "lost 78% in a year", not "fell a lot". SHOWN: the proof itself (the real chart). WRITTEN: the figure.
- Prevents: the generic claim, and disbelief.

**7. State it as fact, take a side.** Say it flat, and stand somewhere a viewer could disagree.
- Why: every "maybe" is a small exit, and a claim exact enough to argue with is where comments come from.
- SAID / WRITTEN: no might, could or probably about your own claim; one stance.
- Prevents: the balanced, hedged opening. It may only be as strong as the facts are (see Honesty).

### C. Why care? (stakes)
**8. It must be theirs.** The viewer finds their own pain, wish or money in the first line.
- Why: a stranger owes you nothing, and "need to have" beats "nice to have".
- SAID: "you" and the consequence first. SHOWN: an ordinary person, or their own situation. WRITTEN: their outcome.
- Prevents: irrelevance. A lecture, a topic ("three trends in..."), a video about its maker.

**9. Someone, something to lose, a clock.** A story says who it is happening to, what is at risk and by when.
- Why: with nothing at risk there is nothing to predict, and a deadline ("within a week") sharpens it.
- SAID: the person, the consequence, the time. SHOWN: the person beside the thing at risk. WRITTEN: the countdown.
- Prevents: facts with nobody in them.

**10. Loss grips harder than gain.** The same fact holds better told as what it cost than as what it won.
- Why: people work harder to avoid a loss than to win a gain, and this account's numbers agree (`memestreet.md`).
- SAID / WRITTEN: the mistake, the cost, the fall, instead of the tip.
- Prevents: the polite how-to. Vary it: fear every day wears out, and the loss has to be real.

**11. One viewer, every time.** Write for the one kind of person the whole account is for.
- Why: the first sample is strangers picked by topic; a hook that pulls another crowd teaches the platform wrongly.
- Do: before wording anything, ask whether this viewer would care about the idea at all. If not, no hook saves it.
- Prevents: a broad hook, a mixed audience, a video stuck at its first sample.

### D. The eye (frame 0)
**12. The eye decides first.** Frame 0 is a thumbnail that is already moving: one subject the viewer recognises,
filling the frame, with words big enough to read.
- Why: people see and read faster than they hear, so the picture is judged before the first word has ended.
- SHOWN: one subject, no clutter, and the motion is the claim itself (the number falling). WRITTEN: three to six
  words, high contrast, inside the safe zone, never over the subject. SAID: the first word inside half a second.
- Prevents: a black or empty first frame, a still card, small pale text, words under the app's buttons.

**13. Look unlike the feed.** The first frame differs from what this viewer has just scrolled past in the same niche.
- Why: the brain filters out what it always sees, and the thumb has to stop before anything can be understood.
- SHOWN: an unusual subject, layout or material. Contrast with the category, not more random motion.
- Prevents: being the hundredth identical video (in finance, a candle chart with a voice over it).

### E. The contract (promise and time)
**14. A hook is a promise.** It sets an expectation that the next line confirms and the video pays in full.
- Why: a viewer who feels tricked leaves, and does not give the next video its three seconds.
- SAID: line two connects to the question the hook planted. SHOWN: the pay-off moment can play under the hook as a
  cold open. WRITTEN: only a claim the video delivers.
- Prevents: clickbait, and a hook that belongs to a different video.

**15. Show the finish line.** The viewer can tell what the end will be and feel it coming.
- Why: people commit to something whose end they can see, and a count or a countdown gives them progress.
- SAID: a foreshadow ("...so I'll prove her wrong with $5"). SHOWN: a counter, a road with an end. WRITTEN: the count.
- Prevents: drifting off in the middle.

**16. Every second is priced.** Nothing is in the opening that is not doing one of the jobs above.
- Why: on a 30-second short one second is 3% of the video, and the skip count closes at 3 seconds.
- SAID: cut lead-ins and filler; no pause. SHOWN: no fade-in, no logo.
- Prevents: throat-clearing at the start, and a tail after the pay-off.

**17. Close one loop, open the next.** Each answer hands over to a new question until the pay-off.
- Why: attention drains unless something is still pending, and facts in a row lose people.
- SAID: "but", "except", "which would have been fine until". Not in every sentence.
- Prevents: hook, then lecture: a good hold with a poor watched share.

## The Triple Hook: the default construction

Kallaway's three steps put the principles in an order. Use it unless the facts refuse it.

1. **Context.** What the video is about, in the first words: said and shown and written, all three where possible
   (principles 1, 2, 3, 12).
2. **Lean.** Something that tips the viewer one way so they lean in instead of opting out: a proof or a number that
   backs the coming claim, a pain or a benefit that is theirs, or something unexpected (4, 6, 8, 9, 10).
3. **Snapback.** A line that pulls against the way they were leaning. "To catch a fish you get the hook in its mouth
   swimming one way, then yank the line the other way." It stays on topic, and a bigger turn pulls harder (5).

His example: "The tech in the Vegas Sphere is insane: biggest screen ever built, 20 times bigger than an IMAX. But get
this: the screen is actually the least impressive part of the whole thing." Meme Street's: "This stock had just landed
UPS. An order for up to 1,000 electric vans. It still lost 78% that year.", with LANDED UPS / LOST 78% on frame 0.

**The test.** Read the first one or two sentences alone, as a stranger would. Is the context clear at once? Is there
something that makes you want the next line? If either answer is no, fix that before anything else.

**How it sits in time.** The voice needs about ten seconds for the three lines and the skip count closes at three, so
the channels share the work:

| When | What has happened |
|---|---|
| frame 0 | The picture is moving and recognisable. The written words already carry the context, at best both sides of the contrast. |
| 0 to 0.5 s | The first spoken word, and it is the subject. |
| by 1.5 s | The number or claim has landed in writing (it may build word by word with the voice). |
| by 3 s | Sentence one is finished: the context and the start of the lean. Something has changed in the picture. |
| 3 to 10 s | The lean completes, the snapback lands, and line two starts paying. |

**When the facts refuse it.** A snapback is a true turn in the material, not a "but" bolted on. If the story has no
turn, lean on a stake, a clock or an open question (9, 4, 15) and say there is no snapback rather than invent one.

## Where the teachers disagree

They do disagree, and each side is right under a condition. Find the condition; do not average the two.

- **"You" or "I".** "You" is right for advice, and for anything the viewer could do or lose themselves. "I" or a named
  person is right for a story, when that person carries a stake the viewer can feel and is ordinary enough to stand in
  for them, or when "I" is the proof. Test: after sentence one, does the viewer know why this matters to them?
- **Hold the pay-off or front-load it.** The topic never waits; only the answer can. When the video is one question (a
  story, a mystery, a challenge), hold the main answer to the end, pay small answers on the way and stop right after
  the pay-off. When it is a list or advice, give a first piece of value straight after the hook and tease the best.
  Either way, an answer that sits visible in the hook kills it.
- **Shock or clarity; name it or hide it.** Clarity is the gate and surprise is the engine. Name the category and hide
  the specific: "this coffee should not taste good" names coffee and hides the flavour. A vague teaser works only when
  the picture or text already gives the topic, or the audience already knows the creator. For strangers it does not.
- **Fear or desire.** A loss frame stops strangers best when they already own the thing at risk (money, a habit, a
  body). A desire frame fits when an outcome is being sold and the viewer can believe it is within reach; the teacher
  who prefers it is talking about buyers. Vary the two across videos.
- **Visual tricks.** One teacher calls visual hooks gimmicks; another says the picture must stop the scroll before a
  word lands. Both reject motion for its own sake. Motion that is the subject or the claim helps; decoration does not.
- **One sentence or three.** Both. Sentence one carries the context and the start of the lean inside 3 seconds; the
  whole hook may run two or three short sentences, to about ten seconds.

Smaller ones (text size, formulas, "if", whom to copy, taking a side, music, endings) are settled the same way in
`references/principles.md`.

## Job 1: WRITE

1. **Understand the video before wording anything.** Write down the one viewer it is for; five to ten true facts it
   holds, each scored by how many of those viewers would not expect it (the single most surprising TRUE fact is the
   hook's raw material); the pay-off and the second it arrives; what must not be given away; and the form (voiced or
   silent, length, platform, the rules of the series). If a fact is missing or unchecked, ask or look it up.
2. **Name the belief and the question.** What does this viewer believe now, and what one question should be in their
   head after the hook? If the question cannot be put in a sentence, there is no hook yet.
3. **Write five hooks from different principles**, not one line reworded. A useful spread: contrast (5), the viewer's
   loss (8 with 10), a person with a stake and a clock (9), an answer hidden in plain sight (4), a finish line or a
   dare (15). Use the Triple Hook wherever the facts hold a true turn.
4. **Give each hook all three channels.** The first two sentences word for word; the words on frame 0 (six at most);
   and the picture at 0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5 and 3.0 s. A silent video has no SAID: the written beats take its
   place and have to do all of its work.
5. **Score each** on the eight checks of the diagnosis below, 0 to 2 each. A 0 on thumb, delay, confusion, agreement
   or promise rules the hook out however clever it is, because those are the gates a viewer passes first.
6. **Pick one and say why**: the principle that carries it, what it trades away and what you are unsure of. Give line
   two, so the body starts paying the promise. Keep the runner-up for the next video; one angle twice in a row tires.

## Job 2: DIAGNOSE

A flop has one first cause and the later faults only inherit it, so look for the first broken thing, in the order a
viewer meets them.

**Evidence first.** Get the first two sentences, what frame 0 showed, the on-screen words, and the numbers: hold or
skip rate at 3 s, watched share or average watch time, views and reach. For Meme Street the ig-stats skill reads them
(`igstats.py reel <words>`). Then weigh them:

- One reel shown to about 100 people has a hold good to about 10 points either way. One reel is **a hint**. Several
  reels of one kind pointing the same way is **likely**. Groups of four or more with a gap beyond the noise is
  **solid**. Use those words in the answer.
- With no numbers at all, say that the diagnosis is a reading of the hook against the principles, not a measurement.
  If frame 0 or the on-screen words are unknown, say which question could not be checked.

**Which stage broke.** A weak hold means the first 3 seconds: questions 1 to 7. A typical hold with a weak watched
share means the hook did its first job and the video let it down: question 8. If both are fine and views are still
low, look at the audience (11), sends and saves, and things outside the video, and do not blame the hook.

**The eight questions.** Stop at the first "no". Each one assumes those before it are fixed.

| # | Check | Ask | A "no" means | Principles |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Thumb | Is frame 0 moving, recognisable, uncluttered, readable and unlike the feed? | Swiped before a word was heard. | 12, 13 |
| 2 | Delay | Is the subject in the first six words, on frame 0 and in the first text? | The viewer could not place it in time. | 1, 16 |
| 3 | Confusion | Read sentence one alone. One reading? Every word a stranger's word? | They did not understand what was on offer. | 2 |
| 4 | Agreement | Do said, shown and written mean the same thing? Does it work muted? | They stalled fitting three messages together. | 3 |
| 5 | Relevance | Whose is it? A stake for this viewer, or a person with something to lose? Is this the account's viewer? | Clear, and not theirs. | 8, 9, 10, 11 |
| 6 | Curiosity | Name the one question it plants. Is it still unanswered on screen? Does it cross something they believe? | A label, a teaser, or a fact they already knew. | 4, 5 |
| 7 | Belief | Is it specific, believable and said flat? | They did not buy the claim. | 6, 7 |
| 8 | Promise | Does line two connect to the question? Does the body pay it when the viewer expects? Is the end in sight? | The hook worked and the video let it down. | 14, 15, 17 |

At question 6, if nothing in the whole video would surprise this viewer, the fault is the idea: say that a new opening
cannot fix it. Name the fault as something visible in the video ("it opened on the label 'Daily Chart #6 recap' over
a still chart"), never as the number said again ("the hook was weak"). Later faults are only what would matter next.

## Job 3: REWRITE

Three rewrites, each fixing the diagnosed fault by a different principle and labelled with it.

- **Keep the facts.** A rewrite changes which true fact leads and how it is framed. It never improves the numbers.
- **A different principle is a different mechanism, not other words.** For a label with no question: one rewrite by
  contrast (5), one by the viewer's loss (8 with 10), one by a stake and a clock (9) or a hidden answer (4).
- **When the fault is in family A or D** (delay, confusion, agreement, the frame), the repair is the same in all three:
  name the subject first, or fix the frame. Make that repair in each and let the three differ in what carries the lean.
- Give each rewrite all three channels and the first 3 seconds, run it through the eight questions, and say what it
  trades away. Then say which to try first and why, and which number would settle it.

## Honesty

A hook is a promise made to a stranger, and the account pays for every one it breaks.

- **The video keeps the promise.** Write the hook from what the video delivers. If the pay-off is weaker than the
  hook, weaken the hook or fix the video.
- **No invented facts.** No made-up numbers, quotes, people, player counts or "90% of people" statistics. A figure in a
  hook comes from the data or a named source. If it could not be checked, say so and leave a marked slot.
- **No deception, even where a lesson recommends it.** The source lessons include a fake comment posted from a second
  account, a deliberate mistake planted to bait comments, and rage lines that the video does not match. None of them
  is in this skill, and none should come back in through a rewrite.
- **A benchmark belongs to someone.** Quote a number with whose channel it came from and what it was measured on
  (`references/numbers.md`). A Shorts creator's 90% retention is not an Instagram target.
- **Say how sure you are.** One reel is a hint. Twelve of the twenty lessons are one creator (Kallaway), so their
  agreement is one voice.
- For Meme Street: a live chart is never spoiled, and every caption carries the practice-money line (`BIBLE.md`).

## With the other skills

- **virality** has an eight-point checklist for the first 3 seconds, built from one account's winners and flops (AI
  tools for Instagram). This skill agrees with it on a moving frame 0, a recognisable subject, one big element, the
  subject spoken within half a second, working on mute and being about the viewer. It differs in three places. Use
  its checklist as a frame-by-frame check once this skill has chosen the hook.
  - *When the words land* (its point 5: "around 1.2 to 1.6 s, never at 0"). That account's frame 0 held two logos
    every viewer knew, so the picture gave the context. Without a known logo, the context words are on screen from
    frame 0; the claim or number can still land near 1.5 s.
  - *"Something extraordinary"* (point 4). Right when the event is the claim made visible. An explosion that says
    nothing fails question 4, and the lessons here warn against motion for its own sake.
  - *"About the viewer, not you"* (point 8). Right for advice. A story with a person at risk holds as well or better;
    this account's best holds are stories.
- **ig-stats** reads the account's numbers and says how far to trust them. Use it for every Meme Street diagnosis.
- **memestreet-reel** and the studio (`src/studio`: `BIBLE.md`, `studio.py lint`) build the reels. Lint checks a hook's
  form by word lists and lengths; it cannot tell whether a stranger cares. That judgement is this skill's.
