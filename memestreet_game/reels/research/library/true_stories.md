# True money stories: the reel library slice

Made 2026-11-22. Short videos that tell a real money story (trades, frauds, rise and fall, founders, money history), each ranked against its own channel's median. `x` is a video's views over the median of the channel's newest Shorts (60 on YouTube, 120 on TikTok). The machine-readable copy is `D:/reel-tmp/lib_true/true_stories.json`.

## 1. The verdict

**The style to copy is the faceless "this man" money story**: one narrator who is never seen tells, in about 50 seconds and about 165 words, how one ordinary person got a concrete sum of money out of a bigger party by an odd means. The picture is a collage of photos and stock footage with the person's name and each figure written big on screen.

Why this one:

- **It wins repeatedly on one channel with the production held constant, and its scripts could be read word for word.** On How Money Works (median 126,500 views over its 50 listed Shorts) six studied stories reached 1.3 to 5.5 million views (x10 to x43). Four others with the same voice, pace, length and pictures stayed at 40,000 to 71,000 (x0.3 to x0.6). What differs between the two groups is the script, so the script can be specified.
- **It is the form of this year's biggest true-story Shorts found.** Shubham Rajput (Hindi voice, median 83,000) has 14 of its 60 newest Shorts at 9x or more; the top two, from February and July 2026, have 27.6 and 11.8 million views. Their Hindi captions show the same skeleton: a "this man" hook, a setup with the year, a small figure, the move, the big figure, a turn (section 5.12).
- **A pipeline without a presenter can make it.** Four of the eleven styles found (C, D, J, K) need a person on camera or a real interview.

Three things to know before copying it:

1. **The six English exemplars are old**: July 2021 to December 2022. Their view counts are four to five years of accumulation. The 2026 evidence is the Hindi channel, read through YouTube's automatic Hindi captions and my own translation.
2. **x is against a channel that already had an audience.** A new account copying the form does not inherit a 126,500 median.
3. **I could not hear anything.** Pace and pauses are computed from YouTube's word-timed captions (about 0.3 s coarse); "music bed" means only that the sound is never quiet.

### 5.3 The hook, exactly

The six first sentences:

1. "This is David Choe, a professional graffiti artist who probably made the smartest decision of his life in 2005"
2. "This man effectively created a free money machine and used it to make over three hundred thousand dollars completely legally and completely tax free."
3. "A Houston resident just made $3,000 by cleverly using a 3D printer to take advantage of a gun buyback event."
4. "Steve Ballmer, currently worth over ninety billion dollars, is the richest person ever to have made his fortune from "a job"."
5. "Every member of Seminole tribe becomes a multi-millionaire the day they turn 18."
6. "This is how British Military Leadership was outsmarted by a bunch of farmers."

The pattern:

- A statement. No question, no "did you know", no "you", no "imagine".
- The grammatical subject is the person or the group. A label unless the person is famous: "this man", "a Houston resident", "a professional graffiti artist".
- It holds the payoff: a sum (2, 3, 4), a superlative (1, 4) or a status (5). Number 6 has only the upset.
- It holds the means in four of six: "a free money machine", "using a 3D printer", "from a job", "outsmarted".
- One judging word in most: "effectively", "cleverly", "probably", "smartest", "completely legally", "outsmarted".
- 15 to 23 words, 4.6 to 8.7 s including the pause after it.

Templates, slots in brackets:

- "This [man / label] [did an impossible-sounding thing] and used it to make over [sum] completely legally."
- "A [place] [resident / label] just made [sum] by cleverly using [an everyday object] to take advantage of [a programme or rule]."
- "This is [Name], a [job] who probably made the smartest decision of his life in [year]."
- "This is how [the powerful party] was outsmarted by a bunch of [ordinary people]."

### 5.4 The ending, exactly

The six last sentences:

1. "If you want to know how to make smart decisions like David, make sure to like and subscribe to keep on learning How Money Works" (the 2021 one; the only call to action among the winners)
2. "The best part is that credit card rewards are not considered income in the eyes of the IRS so Konstantine's profits were all tax free!"
3. "The mastermind of this scheme who wanted to remain anonymous said he didn't do it for the money, but rather to demonstrate just how silly the program was to begin with."
4. "Despite his sales performance he is probably best known by Microsoft employees for the time he held a funeral for the iPhone because he believed the windows phone was going to make the apple product irrelevant."
5. "Tragically teenagers and life changing amounts of money are not a great combination, and the tribe has had serious issues with the money being spent recklessly on fast cars and parties often with devastating results."
6. "Some farmers even harvested the sap before destroying the plants, getting paid twice for the same crop."

The pattern:

- One sentence, 16 to 37 words, 6 to 11 s.
- A further fact about the same people. It raises the story (2, 6) or turns it (3, 4, 5).
- It opens with a turning word: "The best part is", "Some ... even", "Despite", "Tragically", "The mastermind ... said".
- No moral, no lesson, no question to the viewer, no "follow for more" in five of six.
- The reel ends about 1 to 2 s after its last word starts. The 2022 ones end on the story's last picture; the 2021 one closes on the channel's logo.

The description is the whole script, one sentence to a paragraph, nothing else in the part I read.

### 5.6 Text on screen

- **No running subtitles** on the winners I saw at full size (vandal, amex, government, Ballmer, Seminole). The January 2023 flop has small two-line subtitles at the bottom; that is the only one seen with them.
- **Three to six overlays per reel**, each on screen while it is being said:
  - the person's name at first mention ("David Choe");
  - each figure ("$60,000", "$310,000", "5% Cashback", "4%" over "5%", "$50" over "$200", "$90 BILLION", "$700 AN ACRE", "2002");
  - one or two key phrases ("Tax Free" / "Legally", "GHOST GUNS", "THE SIXTH LARGEST CASINO IN THE WORLD").
- Lettering: bold white sans-serif with a soft dark shadow, one to three lines, centred, roughly a third to half the frame wide. Figures are the largest thing on screen.
- One source card in some: a Forbes headline, a tweet.
- The sting gets a joke picture, not a caption: a speech bubble reading "I DIDN'T DO IT FOR THE MONEY", a cut-out of Ballmer as a priest at an iPhone's coffin.

### 5.7 The picture

- The person is on screen within the first half second: from frame 0 as a photo or a clip (vandal, amex), or popping in at 0.5 s as a cut-out over a place or a flat green ground (government, Ballmer, Seminole).
- An unnamed person is a black silhouette with a white question mark, and the silhouette returns whenever he is mentioned.
- The payoff figure is on screen by second 2 to 5 in three of the five seen at full size.
- Then a new picture about every 4 to 6 s (7 to 14 distinct pictures in the 18 sampled frames): stock footage of the thing being named (a card reader, a keyboard, cash being counted, a casino), cut-out collages on the flat ground, logos, one or two meme cutaways.
- Changes are mostly pop-ins, slides and slow zooms, which is why hard cuts count low (0 to 11 in five of the six, 23 in one).
- The narrator is never shown.
- Two of six splice in a real person's sound bite with its picture: an official explaining the buyback (6 s, at 17 s), a film line (1.5 s, at 5 s).

### 5.8 What the same channel's flops changed

Everything in 5.1, 5.6 and 5.7 is the same. The scripts differ:

- **Options (x0.3).** Opens on a definition: "An options contract is an advanced financial instrument that...". The person, Thales, arrives at 18 s. There is no sum of money for him anywhere.
- **Green energy (x0.3).** The same perverse-incentive story as the farmers one (x10.3), told without a person: "This renewable energy plan backfired spectacularly!". Its one figure, $800 million, comes at 90% and is a cost to a government.
- **Printer paper (x0.5).** "a major asset management firm has had to delay a shareholder vote". An institution inconvenienced; ends on a plug for a newsletter.
- **Bank for $1 (x0.6).** HSBC sells a French bank for one euro. Institutions on both sides; the big figure is a $3 billion cost at 30%; ends on a like-and-subscribe line.

So the template only holds when beat 1 names a person or a group of ordinary people who come out ahead, and the big figure is their gain.

### 5.9 The swap to Meme Street's subject

Only the nouns change. Slots:

| WHO | a graffiti artist, a card holder, a Houston resident, farmers | a label: this cleaner, this student, this trader |
| bigger party | Facebook, American Express, a city, an army | a company issuing shares, a broker, an exchange, a fund |

Two limits I can see and did not test: these are real, named, checkable people and sums, and the comments check them; and nothing here says an invented story in this form would hold.
