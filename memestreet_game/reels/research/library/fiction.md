# The fiction slice of the reel library: invented stories, what-ifs, dilemmas, parables (22 November 2026)

One part of the reel library. It covers reels that tell something made up: a what-if, a town with a rule, a dilemma, a parable,
a he-or-she drama. Since the owner's newest instruction is to copy ONE style exactly and change only the subject, the middle of
this file is a **COPY SPEC** for the strongest style here. The machine-readable twin is `fiction.json`.

## The short version

- **The style to copy is the owner's exemplar: Yastor's "What if your reaction time was 10x faster?"**, a voiced 3D simulation
  in the second person. On Instagram it has 231,881 likes. The same film on YouTube has 230,486 views, 38.7 times that
  channel's median, and the channel's two other films in the form have 1,476,582 (235 times) and 748,592 (126 times), on a
  channel of 2,760 subscribers. So strangers carried it, not an audience.
- **It is not a sure thing, and the form alone does not carry it.** 3 of Yastor's 14 Shorts passed five times the median; the
  other 11 sit between 3,200 and 20,000 views, and the three of those that were watched have the same look, the same
  captions, the same turn and the same closing question as the winners. On TikTok all 9 posts sit between 195 and 1,312 views. A second channel in the same family (Alternate Outcomes
  Animations, silent) has 5 of 41 over five times. Together: 8 of 55, about one in seven.
- **Money has done badly in this form, in the three cases seen.** Yastor's "What If Your Money Doubled Every Day?" has 3,932
  views (0.7 times); Alternate Outcomes' jackpot and bank-vault films have 0.2 times each. The same doubling-penny subject told
  as a would-you-rather by a live presenter (Prince Ea) has 48.4 million views, 317 times his median. Three cases are a warning,
  not a law, but the owner's plan (this form, money as the subject) is the one combination in the sample with no win yet.
- **What the three winners share that the three watched flops lack** is the subject and the claims, not the structure: the
  subject is something every viewer owns or belongs to (their own reflexes, their country), and the claims are real
  figures and dated facts, shown with their source, that people can argue with. The top comments on the winners are corrections
  and additions (5,200 likes on one); on the flops, "holy ai slop".
- The runner-up for a money subject is the retold parable (DuckModo: 755,838 views, 60 times its median, on 2,330
  subscribers), but only one was watched, so there is no copy spec for it.

## What was done

- **Listed, numbers and titles only: 35,062 short videos (three minutes or less).** Three listings: 121 searches and the channels behind them
  (`lib_fiction.json`, on the server), 111 story searches with YouTube's under-four-minutes filter (`lib_fiction2.json`), and
  45 story hashtags from this PC with the channels behind their biggest Shorts (`tags.json`, `lib_tags.json`). 24,569 of them,
  from 644 channels, are ranked against their own channel's median (its newest 40 Shorts): 10,324 have 100,000 views or
  more, 4,560 a million or more, 3,468 are over five times their channel.
- **Watched: 26 Shorts and the owner's Instagram reel.** Each fetched small, cut into a sheet of 18 frames (24 for the
  Instagram reel), its hard cuts counted, its captions read with their times; nothing kept but the sheet and the notes
  (`D:/reel-tmp/lib_fiction/sheets`, `.../notes`). 18 more were looked at through YouTube's public stills only.
  On-screen words quoted below were read off those small frames.
- **Word-timed transcripts: six**, all Yastor (three winners, three flops), from YouTube's own caption track, which carries a
  time on every word.
- **Limits.** Instagram shows likes but not views without a login, and its profiles cannot be listed, so there is no
  "times the account's median" for Instagram. YouTube answered the watch lists with its bot check twice; the lists were
  stopped, not forced, so the kindness tales and Bloopo are thin (one film each). The transcripts are YouTube's hearing of the
  voice: the wording is the film's, the punctuation and the shorthand for figures ("3 mm" for "three millimetres") are
  YouTube's. Times are word starts, good to about a quarter of a second. Every "x" is against the channel's own median, so a
  small channel's 235x and a large channel's 4x are both real and not the same thing.

### The skeleton, as rules

1. **Hook: one sentence, 6 to 14 words, done by 3.2 to 3.5 s** (6.0 s for the two-clause one). It names the subject and the
   size of the change, in the present tense, and the event is already under way in the first frame.
2. **Sentence 2 is the first result**, starts at 3.2 to 6.0 s, 5 to 7 words, on the object the viewer is already looking at.
3. **A rising list of 7 to 9 results, 115 to 132 words, over the next 45 to 55 s.** Each result is 1 to 5 sentences and 2 to
   9 s: the thing, a real figure or date or name, then a punchline of 4 words or fewer ("Now it's just bread." "Caught."
   "Sorry Russia." "Built."). They are ordered from the hand to the world: a phone, toast, keys, wine, a banknote, a game,
   the Olympics; a bagel, vitamins, vodka, then the periodic table, the sun, the war.
4. **The turn: one sentence of 4 to 8 words that starts with "But", at 62%, 68% and 73% of the length** (59.3 s of 96,
   55.5 of 82, 49.3 of 68). In two of the three it follows the longest silence of the film (1.8 s).
5. **After the turn, 27 to 54 words in 12 to 23 s:** the one thing that outweighs the list (the cost, the biggest loss, the
   comeback). It opens with long sentences (11 to 17 words; in one film the longest of all) and then goes short again.
6. **A callback to the viewer:** the first object ("You'd never drop your phone again."), the viewer's own phone ("So, how
   are you watching this?"), the film itself ("And this video for you, it lasted over 15 minutes.").
7. **The question: the last 4 to 9 words, a yes-or-no or a pick of one**, starting 1.7 to 5 s before the file ends. One film
   adds a 17-word joke after the question (its question starts 9.8 s before the end).

### The sentences

- 24 to 41 sentences. The median sentence is 4 to 6 words; the longest is 13 to 17: the chip sentence right after the turn
  (17), the stork sentence (14), the Olympic rule (13).
- The master's device is **question, then answer**: 13 of its 41 sentences are questions of 1 to 6 words ("Toast?" "Keys over
  the drain?" "Blaming the lag?" "For you?" "An 8-hour shift?") answered in 1 to 3 words ("Butter side up." "Caught." "80.").
  18 of its 41 sentences are 3 words or fewer. The Poland films use it less (3 and 4 questions).
- **Figures.** In the master and in Poland-gone about a third of the sentences carry a figure (15 of 41,
  8 of 24). Poland-invented has fewer (7 of 30) and gives a name or a place in every item
  instead (Krakow, Max Factor, Polonium, Copernicus, Enigma). The first figure is in sentence 1 or 2 in the master ("10
  times", then "3 mm" by 4.8 s) and comes at 6.0 s and 10.6 s in the other two. The figures are real ones and the source
  is on screen in small type (see On screen). The master pairs each figure with its normal: a quarter of a second
  against a tenth of it, 30 cm against 3 mm, 24 frames against a slideshow, one minute against ten.
- Second person, present tense, throughout: "your phone", "you catch them", "you'd have". Nobody in the film has a name.

## Changing only the subject to money

What stays fixed is everything above: 13 beats, about 237 words in about 96 s (or the Poland length: 160 to 205 words in

What the record says about the subject (a reading of six films, not a measurement):

- Make it **the viewer's own** money in everyday acts, the way the master is the viewer's own hand: their pay, their rent,
  the price on the shelf in front of them. Not a deal, a jackpot or a vault.
- Every result needs **a real figure with a normal beside it and a source under it**. A sum that anyone can do in their head
  is not a claim anyone argues with.
- The turn should be **a cost the first half set up** (the master plants "slow motion" in beat 8 and pays it in beat 10).
- Premises of that shape, untested: "What if every price dropped 10% tomorrow?", "What if your pay doubled, and so did
  everyone's?", "What if you could see every price one day early?", "What if nothing you own could lose value?"
